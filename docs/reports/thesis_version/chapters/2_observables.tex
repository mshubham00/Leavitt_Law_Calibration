\section{Why Does a Star Shine?}

The luminosity of a star originates from nuclear fusion reactions occurring in its core. During star formation, gravitational collapse of interstellar gas leads to extremely high central pressures and densities, raising the core temperature to several million kelvin. Under these conditions, thermal energies become sufficient to overcome the Coulomb repulsion between protons, enabling nuclear fusion.

In the Sun and other low-mass main-sequence stars, energy production is dominated by the proton--proton (pp) chain, in which hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium through a sequence of reactions discussed in the following chapter. Approximately $7 \times 10^{8}$~metric tons of hydrogen are converted into helium each second, releasing energy according to mass--energy equivalence.

A fraction of this energy is carried away by neutrinos, which escape the stellar interior almost unimpeded, while the remainder is transported outward by radiation and convection and ultimately emitted from the stellar surface as electromagnetic radiation. This radiation spans a broad range of wavelengths, with the peak emission in the optical band for solar-type stars, and propagates through space as starlight.

\section{Luminosity}

Light is electromagnetic (EM) radiation that propagates in vacuum at a constant speed of
$c = 2.99792458 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The human eye is sensitive only to a narrow portion of the EM spectrum—the optical band—spanning approximately $350$--$700$ nm in wavelength. This range represents a small subset of the full electromagnetic spectrum, which extends from high-energy gamma rays to long-wavelength radio emission.

\input{chapters/content/0_2_plot_spectrum.tex}

\input{chapters/content/0_2_plot_blackbody.tex}

The spectral distribution of radiation emitted by matter is governed by its thermal state. An idealized emitter, known as a blackbody, absorbs all incident radiation and re-emits it with a spectral energy distribution (SED) determined solely by its temperature. With increasing thermal excitation, the emission peak shifts toward shorter wavelengths, while the total radiative output increases. This behavior is quantitatively described by Planck’s law, which underpins modern radiative theory.

According to \cite{1900planck}, the monochromatic specific intensity of blackbody radiation describes the radiative power emitted per unit frequency (or wavelength), per unit area, per unit solid angle, by a blackbody in thermal equilibrium. It depends only on the frequency (or wavelength) and the temperature of the emitter and is given by Planck’s law,
\begin{equation}
B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2 h c^2}{\lambda^5} \left( e^{\frac{h c}{\lambda k T}} - 1 \right)^{-1}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength, $T$ the temperature, $h$ Planck’s constant, and $k$ Boltzmann’s constant and $B_\lambda$ is the specific intensity in units of $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,m^{-1}}$, .

Integrating it over the outward hemisphere above the blackbody surface gives emergent monochromatic flux $F_\lambda$

\begin{equation}
F_\lambda(T) = \int B_\lambda(T)\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = \pi B_\lambda(T).
\end{equation}

The total radiative flux is obtained by integrating the spectral flux density over all wavelengths,
\begin{equation}
F = \int_{0}^{\infty} F_\lambda \, \mathrm{d}\lambda = \sigma T^{4},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the Stefan--Boltzmann constant.



Consider a star of radius $R$ emitting a surface flux $F_\lambda$. At a distance $d$ from the star, the observed monochromatic flux is reduced according to the inverse-square law,
\begin{equation}
f_\lambda = \left( \frac{R}{d} \right)^2 F_\lambda .
\end{equation}

A detector measures radiation over a finite wavelength range defined by its instrumental response (or sensitivity) function $E_\lambda$. The detected signal is therefore
\begin{equation}
s = \int_0^\infty f_\lambda E_\lambda \, \mathrm{d}\lambda .
\end{equation}

In observational astronomy, this integrated flux is recorded within discrete wavelength intervals,
referred to as \textit{photometric bands}, and is commonly expressed in logarithmic units known as \textit{magnitudes}.

\subsection{Magnitude Scale}
The ancient Greek astronomer Hipparchus (c. 190 – c. 120 BCE) compiled a star catalogue and introduced a scale of stellar brightness in units of magnitude (mag), where 1~mag corresponds to the brightest stars visible to the unaided eye, and 6~mag corresponds to the dimmest. The magnitude scale was revisited by Pogson in the 1850s \cite{1856pogson} and later calibrated by Zöllner in 1861 using a visual photometer \cite{1861zoellner}. In their work, comparisons were made between artificial and real stars using two Nicol prisms to establish a more precise magnitude scale.  

According to Pogson's definition, the difference in apparent magnitudes between two stars is proportional to the logarithm of their radiation flux ratio ($s_1/s_2$), i.e.,

\begin{equation}
m_1 - m_2 = 2.5 \log\left(\frac{s_1}{s_2}\right).
\end{equation}

Thus, a difference of 5 magnitudes corresponds to a brightness ratio of 100. For example, $\alpha$~Lyr (Vega) has a visual magnitude of 0.14~mag, while $\alpha$~Cyg (Deneb) has 1.33~mag, making it fainter than Vega. The Sun, Moon, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and three stars—Sirius, Canopus, and $\alpha$~Centauri—are the only celestial objects with negative apparent magnitudes, placing them among the brightest objects in the night sky.


\subsection{Color Index \textit{($m_1 - m_2$)}}

The difference between stellar magnitudes measured in two distinct wavelength bands is termed the \emph{color index}. Because stellar flux varies with wavelength, the measured magnitude of a star depends on the observational filter employed, leading to observable color differences among stars.

As an illustration, Betelgeuse appears brighter than Sirius when observed through a red filter, despite Sirius being the brightest star in the visual band. This contrast reflects the differing spectral energy distributions of the two stars: Sirius emits most strongly at shorter wavelengths, whereas Betelgeuse radiates predominantly at longer wavelengths. Such variations arise from differences in their photospheric effective temperatures.

The physical basis for interpreting stellar colors was established by Karl Schwarzschild, who demonstrated that the shape of a stellar spectral energy distribution is primarily governed by temperature. His analysis showed that color measurements provide a quantitative means of estimating stellar photospheric temperatures \citep{1900schwarzschild}.

Building upon this theoretical framework, W.~W.~Morgan and H.~L.~Johnson developed a standardized photometric system to measure stellar colors and magnitudes reliably. The Johnson--Morgan UBV system consists of three broadband filters with effective wavelengths in the ultraviolet ($\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = 365$~nm), blue ($\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = 440$~nm), and visual ($\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = 548$~nm) regions \citep{1953johnson}. This system was subsequently extended into the red and infrared through the addition of further passbands, commonly specified in micrometers ($\mu$m):

\[
\begin{array}{ccccccccccccc}
\text{Band} & U & B & V & R & I & J & H & K & L & M & N & Q \\
\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}~(\mu\mathrm{m}) & 
0.36 & 0.44 & 0.55 & 0.70 & 0.90 & 1.25 & 1.63 & 2.20 & 3.60 & 5.00 & 10.6 & 21
\end{array}
\]

By combining broadband photometry with spectral classification, Morgan further developed the MK system, which allows stars to be classified simultaneously by effective temperature (spectral type) and luminosity class. These advances established color indices as a fundamental observational tool in stellar astrophysics, providing a quantitative link between stellar color and physical properties.

The stellar energy distribution can be approximated by Planck’s radiation law. Adopting Wien’s approximation \(\left( c_2/(\lambda T) \gg 1 \right)\), the monochromatic radiative flux is given by
\begin{equation}
F_\lambda \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{c_2}{\lambda T_{\mathrm{eff}}}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(c_2 = hc/k_B \) is the second radiation constant equivalent to = 1.4388 cm K.

Corresponding magnitude would be,
\begin{equation}
m_\lambda = -2.5 \log F_\lambda + const.
\end{equation}

Assuming that the detector sensitivity peaks near the effective wavelengths of the photometric filters, the relation between the color index \(B-V\) ($m_B - m_V$) and the stellar effective temperature can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
B - V = \frac{2.5\, c_2 \log e}{T_{\mathrm{eff}}}
\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_B} - \frac{1}{\lambda_V} \right) + \mathrm{const.}
\end{equation}

Using \(c_2 = 0.014~\mathrm{m\,K}\), \(\lambda_B = 440~\mathrm{nm}\), and \(\lambda_V = 548~\mathrm{nm}\), this reduces to
\begin{equation}
B - V \approx \frac{0.7 \times 10^{4}}{T_{\mathrm{eff}}} + \mathrm{const.}
\end{equation}

This expression clearly demonstrates that the color index \(B-V\) provides a direct estimate of a star's effective temperature, highlighting the utility of photometric measurements in stellar astrophysics.

Adopting \(\alpha\)~Lyr (Vega; spectral type A0V) as the standard star, the photometric zero points in the \(U\), \(B\), and \(V\) bands are defined such that
\begin{equation}
U - B = B - V = 0,
\end{equation}
implying \(C_{UB} = C_{BV} = 0\).

For convenience, the color index \(U-B\) is denoted by \(C_{UB}\). Stars with $C_{BV} \lesssim 0.5$ are classified as blue, while those with $C_{BV} \gtrsim 1.5$ are considered red.

\subsection{Absolute Magnitude (\textit{M})}
When a luminous object is observed from different distances, it appears to have varying brightness because flux decreases with increasing distance. So, how can we compare the intrinsic brightness of two remote stars?  To enable standardized comparisons, astronomers adopt a reference distance of 10 parsecs (pc). The radiant flux of a star measured at this distance defines its \textit{absolute magnitude}, denoted as $M_\lambda$. The subscript $\lambda$ indicates the specific photometric band (filter) used in the observation, as brightness varies across different bands.

Given this framework, if $m_\lambda$ represents the apparent magnitude of a star observed at a distance \textit{d} (in parsecs), and $M_\lambda$ is its absolute magnitude, the relationship between them is given by the following formula:

$$M_\lambda = m_\lambda - 2.5\log \left( \frac{d[pc]}{10}\right)^2 $$
\begin{align}
M_\lambda = m_\lambda - 5\log{d[pc]}  + 5 
\end{align}

The above is a simple relation which relates the observable luminosity with the distance of any stellar object. 

\begin{notebox}[sharp corners, width=\textwidth]{True Absolute Magnitude}
The above relation must be corrected for the effect of interstellar extinction, $A_\lambda$, to obtain the correct \textit{'photometric'} distance. 
\begin{align}
M_\lambda^0 = m_\lambda - A_\lambda - 5\log{d[pc]}  + 5 
\end{align}
\end{notebox}

\section{Interstellar Reddening}

In photometric observations, the electromagnetic flux of starlight is quantified by counting the incoming photons within a specific wavelength range of the spectrum. Intervening gas in interstellar space absorbs most of the high-energy photons (X-rays and extreme ultraviolet) especially and scatters much of the light in the B and V bands. Radiation with wavelengths longer than the size of dust particles can pass through the interstellar medium largely unaffected, making the sky nearly transparent to infrared, microwave, and radio waves. The diminishing of short-wavelength luminosity makes the source appear redder than its intrinsic color, giving rise to the effect known as reddening.


\input{chapters/content/0_2_plot_extinction.tex}


This phenomenon can be illustrated by comparing the observed SED of stellar light to the theoretical SED predicted by Planck's Law, as shown in Figure \ref{extinction}. \\
Let B and V be the apparent luminosities of a star as received by the observer, while $B_0$ and $V_0$ represent the unextincted luminosities. Then, the effect of extinction can be formulated as follows:
\begin{align}
A_B = B - B_0 \\
A_V = V - V_0 
\end{align} 

From the plot, it can be noticed that B band experienced higher extinction than V, skewing the SED towards red. The difference between the two extinctions termed as \textit{color excess} - that is, the deviation of a star's apparent color from its intrinsic color. 
\begin{align}
E(B-V) = A_B - A_V = (B - V) - (B - V)_0 
\end{align} 

Color excess is more widely used than extinction to describe the effect of the ISM on starlight, as it can be measured photometrically and do not depend on the measurement of distance to the target. 

\subsection{Reddening Ratio}
The ratio of total-to-selective absorption, $R_\lambda$, summarizes the effect of extinction and reddening on the intensity of light along the line-of-sight. It is formulated as:

\begin{align}\label{eq:reddening_ratio}
R_\lambda^{BV} = \frac{A_\lambda}{E(B-V)}
\end{align}

For Milky Way, the reddening ratio in V-band, $R_V$, is evaluated about 3.23 \citep{2004sandage}, whereas in B-band it set to be as $R_B$ = $R_V + 1$

Rearranging the above relation and writting it as:
\begin{align}\label{eq:extinction}
	A_\lambda =& R_\lambda E_{BV} = R_\lambda (A_B - A_V) 
\end{align}
The above equation is used for transforming measured color excess into extinction for any band, provided $R_\lambda$ is priorily known.  Rearranging it further, for V-band, the equation takes the form:
\begin{align}
	\frac{A_B}{A_V} = & 1 + \frac{1}{R_V}
\end{align}

\cite{1989cardelli} generalized the extinction law in this form for all the bands with V-band as reference as discussed in the following section. 

An another important aspect of the reddening ratio is to convert color excess from one color basis, say $E_{BV}$ to another, $E_{1 2}$. It can be done as follows:

\begin{align*}
E_{12} = (R_{1}^{BV} - R_{2}^{BV}) E_{BV} 
\end{align*}


This leads the transformation law for reddening ratio $R_\lambda$:

\begin{align}\label{R_transform}
    R_\lambda^{12} = \frac{R_\lambda^{BV}}{R_1^{BV} - R_2^{BV}}
\end{align}

\subsection{Wesenheit Function: Reddening-free Magnitude}

To mitigate the effects of interstellar extinction, \citet{1982madore} introduced a multiband pseudo-magnitude based on the reddening ratio, termed as the \emph{Wesenheit magnitude}. By construction, this quantity is insensitive to interstellar reddening, provided that the reddening law is known accurately. Its formulation proceeds as follows.

The reddening ratio is defined as

\begin{align}
R_\lambda^{12} &= \frac{A_\lambda}{E_{12}} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{m_\lambda - m_\lambda^0}{(m_1 - m_2) - (m_1 - m_2)_0},
\end{align}
where $m_\lambda$ and $m_\lambda^0$ are the apparent and unreddened magnitudes, respectively.

Rearranging the above expression yields
\begin{align}
m_\lambda - R_\lambda^{12}(m_1 - m_2)
= m_\lambda^0 - R_\lambda^{12}(m_1 - m_2)_0.
\end{align}
All terms on the left-hand side, except for $R_\lambda^{12}$, are observable quantities. The equality demonstrates that this linear combination of apparent magnitudes is equivalent to the corresponding intrinsic quantity, implying that it is unaffected by reddening. Consequently, if the reddening law is known with sufficient precision, the Wesenheit magnitude attains identical values for reddened and unreddened sources, thereby constituting a reddening-free observable.

\input{chapters/content/0_2_note_wesenheit.tex}

A comparison between the definitions of the intrinsic absolute magnitude and the Wesenheit magnitude clarifies their respective dependencies:
\begin{align}\label{mag_eq}
M_\lambda^0 &= m_\lambda - R_\lambda^{BV} E(B-V) - \mu,
\end{align}
\begin{align}\label{wes_eq}
W_\lambda^{BV} &= m_\lambda - R_\lambda^{BV}(B-V) - \mu,
\end{align}
where $\mu$ denotes the distance modulus.

The absolute magnitude depends on four independent quantities: apparent magnitude, reddening ratio, color excess, and distance modulus. In contrast, the Wesenheit magnitude depends only on the apparent magnitude, reddening ratio, and distance modulus, and is independent of the color excess. Although defined for a specific color index, the Wesenheit formalism naturally forms a family of reddening-free magnitudes. For example, BVIJHK photometry allows fifteen independent color combinations, yielding fifteen Wesenheit families per band and a total of ninety reddening-free magnitudes derived from six apparent magnitudes.

For a given photometric band, consistency among the values obtained from different Wesenheit families is expected if the adopted extinction law is correct. This property provides a means to test and calibrate the reddening law using multi-band photometry, an approach that is applied to the present dataset and discussed further in Chapter~4.

Finally, the propagation of measurement uncertainties in absolute and Wesenheit magnitudes is considered. With multi-epoch photometry, the apparent magnitudes can be determined with negligible uncertainty ($\delta m_\lambda = 0$), and assuming an accurate reddening law ($\delta R_\lambda = 0$), the dominant sources of error are the distance modulus ($\delta \mu$) and the color excess ($\delta E(B-V)$). From Eqs.~\eqref{mag_eq} and~\eqref{wes_eq}, one obtains
\begin{align}\label{mag_error}
\delta M_\lambda &= -\left(R_\lambda^{BV}\,\delta E(B-V) + \delta \mu\right),
\end{align}
\begin{align}\label{wesen_error}
\delta W_\lambda^{BV} &= -\delta \mu.
\end{align}

Both absolute and Wesenheit magnitudes are equally sensitive to uncertainties in the distance modulus. However, the Wesenheit magnitude is immune to errors in the color excess, a property that is central to this work as it enables a clean separation between distance- and reddening-related uncertainties for individual Cepheids.

Accurate distance measurements are essential in astronomy, as derived physical properties and our understanding of Galactic and cosmic structure depend critically on them. Because no single method is valid across all distance scales, different techniques are employed depending on the target and spatial regime. This section describes two geometrical distance determination methods—trigonometric parallax, the Infrared Surface Brightness (IRSB) technique and cluster membership method — used to infer distances to Galactic Cepheid variable stars. 

\subsection{Parallax Method}

Trigonometric parallax is one of the most fundamental techniques for astronomical distance determination. In a right-angled triangle, knowledge of one side and a non-right angle uniquely determines the remaining sides through trigonometric relations. Since these relations depend only on angular measurements and not on the absolute scale of the triangle, they are particularly well suited to astronomical applications where direct distance measurements are infeasible.

\input{chapters/content/0_2_plot_parallax.tex}

In stellar parallax, a conceptual triangle is defined by the Earth, the Sun, and a nearby star. The baseline of this triangle is the Earth--Sun separation, equal to 1 astronomical unit (AU). As the Earth moves along its orbit, observations taken six months apart reveal an apparent shift in the position of the star relative to distant background sources. This angular displacement, known as the parallax angle, is extremely small and is therefore measured in milliarcseconds (mas).

The distance to a star can then be determined from the measured parallax angle using the tangent trigonometric relation:

\begin{equation}
\tan p = \frac{1 AU}{d}
\end{equation}

For very small angles (as in astronomical observations), $\tan p$ is approximately equal to $p$ when measured in radians. Therefore, the distance formula can be simplified to:

\begin{equation}
d = \lim_{p \to 0} \frac{1 AU}{\tan p} = \frac{1 AU}{p} 
\end{equation}

To standardize distance measurements, astronomers use the parsec as a unit of length.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{center}
\textit{1 parsec (pc) is defined as the distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle of 1 arcsecond (1/3600 of a degree).}
\end{center}
\vspace{0.3cm}

In this thesis, parallax-based distances from \textit{Gaia} are adopted; hence, it is important to consider systematic uncertainties. Being bright yellow giants, Cepheid variables saturate CCD pixels, leading to uncertain parallax measurements \cite{2018Lindegren}. The marginal impact of these parallax uncertainties on the resulting Leavitt Law is discussed in a later chapter.

\subsection{Infrared Surface Brightness Method}

The Infrared Surface Brightness (IRSB) method is a geometrical technique belonging to the broader class of \emph{parallax of pulsation} methods. Its fundamental principle is to determine the distance to a Cepheid by comparing its \emph{linear radius variation}, inferred from radial velocity measurements, with its \emph{angular diameter variation}, derived from photometry.

For a spherical star at distance $d$, the basic relation is
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{\text{linear diameter}}{\text{angular diameter}} .
\end{equation}


The IRSB method has its roots in the classical Baade--Becker--Wesselink (BBW) technique \cite{1926baade,1940Becker,1946wesselink}, which exploits the existence of a linear relation between surface brightness and stellar color. \cite{1976barnes} were the first to apply this method to Cepheids using optical photometry (V and $V\!-\!R$) for determining their distances. 

\input{chapters/content/0_2_plot_brightness_color.tex}


In the Barnes--Evans formulation, the visual surface brightness parameter $F_V$ is defined such that
\begin{equation}
V_0 = F_V - 5 \log R + C ,
\end{equation}
where $V_0$ is the dereddened apparent magnitude, $R$ is the stellar radius (in solar units), and $C$ is a constant that depends on the choice of units.

The surface brightness parameter is empirically found to depend linearly on colour,
\begin{equation}
F_V = a\,(V-R)_0 + b ,
\end{equation}
where $(V-R)_0$ is the dereddened colour index.

Let the stellar radius vary with pulsation phase $\phi$ as
\begin{equation}
R(\phi) = R_0 + \Delta R(\phi),
\end{equation}
where $R_0$ is the mean radius. At two pulsation phases $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ with identical color $(V-K)_0$, the surface brightness parameter $F_V$ is the same. Therefore,
\begin{align}
V_1 &= F_V - 5 \log (R_0 + \Delta R_1) + C , \\
V_2 &= F_V - 5 \log (R_0 + \Delta R_2) + C ,
\end{align}
\input{chapters/content/0_2_plot_color_D.tex}

Subtracting the two equations gives
\begin{equation}
V_2 - V_1 = 5 \log \left( \frac{R_0 + \Delta R_1}{R_0 + \Delta R_2} \right).
\end{equation}

Defining $H = 10^{0.2\,(V_2 - V_1)}$, the mean radius can be written as
\begin{equation}
R_0 = \frac{H\,\Delta R_2 - \Delta R_1}{1 - H}.
\end{equation}

The linear radius variation $\Delta R(\phi)$ is obtained independently from spectroscopic radial velocity measurements. The observed radial velocity $v_r(t)$ is converted into the pulsational velocity of the photosphere $v_p(t)$ using the projection factor $p$,
\begin{equation}
v_p(t) = p \,[v_r(t) - v_\gamma],
\end{equation}
where $v_\gamma$ is the systemic velocity. The projection factor accounts for geometric projection effects, limb darkening, atmospheric velocity gradients, and phase-dependent photospheric motion.

The radial displacement is obtained by integrating the pulsation velocity,
\begin{equation}
\Delta R = \int_{t_0}^{t} v_p(t')\,\mathrm{d}t' .
\end{equation}

Since Cepheid pulsation is periodic with period $P$, it is convenient to express the integration in terms of pulsation phase,
\begin{equation}
\phi = \frac{t - t_0}{P}, \qquad \mathrm{d}t = P\,\mathrm{d}\phi ,
\end{equation}
leading to
\begin{equation}
\Delta R(\phi) = p P \int_{0}^{\phi} [v_r(\phi') - v_\gamma]\,\mathrm{d}\phi' .
\end{equation}

The angular diameter can also be written geometrically as

\begin{equation}
\theta  = \frac{2(R_0 +  \Delta R(\phi))}{d} 
\end{equation}

\[
\theta(\phi) = 10^{\,4.2207 - 0.1\,V_0(\phi) - F_V(\phi)}
\]


\begin{equation}
\theta(\phi)  = \frac{2 R_0}{d} +  \frac{2 \Delta R(\phi)}{d} 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\theta(\phi) = \frac{2 R_0}{d} + \frac{2 p P}{d}
\int_{0}^{\phi} [v_r(\phi') - v_\gamma]\,\mathrm{d}\phi' .
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is expressed in milliarcseconds.

This expression is linear and may be written as
\begin{equation}
\theta(\phi) = A + B\,X(\phi),
\end{equation}
where both $\theta(\phi)$ and $X(\phi)$ are directly derived from observations. The slope $B$ yields the distance,
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{2 p P}{B}.
\end{equation}


Subsequent improvements were achieved by adopting the $(V-K)$ colour \cite{1994Welch}, which provides a larger temperature leverage: the V band lies near the Wien regime of Cepheid spectral energy distributions, while the K band lies on the Rayleigh--Jeans tail, making the color primarily sensitive to effective temperature. In addition, extinction effects are significantly reduced in the K band.

A tight empirical calibration between $(V-K)$ and the surface brightness parameter $F_V$, based on interferometric angular diameter measurements, was established by \cite{1997Fouque}. This calibration forms the basis of the modern Infrared Surface Brightness (IRSB) method, which was further refined and applied to Cepheids by \cite{2011storm}.


\subsection{Cluster Membership Method}

Consider an open star cluster containing a Classical Cepheid variable.
All bona fide cluster members share a common true parallax
$\pi_{\mathrm{cl}}$.
Let $N$ cluster stars have measured parallaxes $\{\pi_i\}_{i=1}^N$.

Each parallax measurement is modeled as
\begin{equation}
\pi_i = \pi_{\mathrm{cl}} + \epsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where the errors $\epsilon_i$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_i] = 0,
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma_i^2,
\end{equation}
and are assumed to be statistically independent and Gaussian.

The goal is to estimate $\pi_{\mathrm{cl}}$ with minimum uncertainty and
assign this distance to the Cepheid.

Likelihood Function

The joint likelihood of observing the parallaxes $\{\pi_i\}$ given
$\pi_{\mathrm{cl}}$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\pi_{\mathrm{cl}})
=
\prod_{i=1}^N
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_i^2}}
\exp\!\left[
-\frac{(\pi_i - \pi_{\mathrm{cl}})^2}{2\sigma_i^2}
\right].
\end{equation}

Taking the logarithm,
\begin{equation}
\ln \mathcal{L}
=
-\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^N
\left[
\frac{(\pi_i - \pi_{\mathrm{cl}})^2}{\sigma_i^2}
+ \ln(2\pi\sigma_i^2)
\right].
\end{equation}

The logarithmic normalization term does not depend on
$\pi_{\mathrm{cl}}$ and may be ignored for maximization.

Maximum Likelihood Estimator

Differentiating the log-likelihood with respect to $\pi_{\mathrm{cl}}$
and setting the derivative to zero yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \ln \mathcal{L}}{\partial \pi_{\mathrm{cl}}}
=
\sum_{i=1}^N
\frac{\pi_i - \pi_{\mathrm{cl}}}{\sigma_i^2}
= 0.
\end{equation}

Rearranging terms,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{\pi_i}{\sigma_i^2}
=
\pi_{\mathrm{cl}}
\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}.
\end{equation}

Solving for $\pi_{\mathrm{cl}}$, the maximum-likelihood estimator is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{cl}}
=
\frac{\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^N \pi_i / \sigma_i^2}
{\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^N 1 / \sigma_i^2}
}
\end{equation}
which is the weighted mean parallax.



The expectation value of the estimator is
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{cl}}]
&=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[\pi_i]/\sigma_i^2}
{\sum_{i=1}^N 1/\sigma_i^2} \\
&=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \pi_{\mathrm{cl}}/\sigma_i^2}
{\sum_{i=1}^N 1/\sigma_i^2}
=
\pi_{\mathrm{cl}}.
\end{align}

Thus, the estimator is unbiased.

Variance of the Estimator

Define weights $w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2$.
The estimator can be written as
\begin{equation}
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{cl}} = \frac{\sum_i w_i \pi_i}{\sum_i w_i}.
\end{equation}

The variance is
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{cl}})
&=
\frac{\sum_i w_i^2 \sigma_i^2}{(\sum_i w_i)^2} \\
&=
\frac{\sum_i (1/\sigma_i^2)}{(\sum_i 1/\sigma_i^2)^2} \\
&=
\boxed{
\left( \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} \right)^{-1}
}.
\end{align}

Hence, the standard deviation is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\hat{\pi}} =
\left( \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} \right)^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}



By the Gauss--Markov theorem, among all linear unbiased estimators of
$\pi_{\mathrm{cl}}$, the weighted mean with weights
$w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2$ has the minimum variance.

Therefore, $\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{cl}}$ is the optimal estimator.



The cluster distance is obtained from the parallax as
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{1}{\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{cl}}},
\end{equation}
with fractional uncertainty
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sigma_d}{d}
\approx
\frac{\sigma_{\hat{\pi}}}{\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{cl}}}.
\end{equation}

Since the Cepheid is a cluster member, this distance applies directly to
the Cepheid and can be used to calibrate the period--luminosity relation.

The cluster Cepheid parallax method follows rigorously from maximum
likelihood estimation under Gaussian errors.
The weighted mean parallax is unbiased, minimum-variance, and provides a
high-precision geometric distance to Cepheid variables.


\section{Distance Modulus ($\mu$)}

For the ease of calculation, a dimensionless measure of distance is used in astronomy called as the distance modulus, $\mu$, which provides a logarithmic transformation of the distance to an object, by setting 10 parsecs as the reference distance. It can also be expressed as the difference between the apparent magnitude and the absolute magnitude of an object as the absolute magnitude is the measure of luminosity at the 10 pc distance from the source. Mathematically, distance modulus can be formulated as:

$$\mu = m - M = 5 log_{10}\left[\frac{d}{10 pc}\right] $$
