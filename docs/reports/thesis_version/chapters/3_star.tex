\section{Are all stars the same?}
Seeing the night sky, one can easily spot that stars come in different colors and luminosities, making it easy to infer that not all stars are the same. But what makes them appear different? With the advent of quantum mechanics and spectroscopic observation, this question was answered by explaining the physical processes occurring inside the star, along with changing chemical potential over the different evolutionary phases of the stars. It has been observed that some stars can be thousands of times larger than others—such as red giants and supergiants — while some undergo radial oscillations periodically, like RR Lyrae, Cepheids, and Mira variables. Some stars may form diffuse gaseous systems, such as protostars and planetary nebulae, while others could be the remnants of dead stars, like white dwarfs or neutron stars. These examples represent different phases in the life cycle of a star. This chapter delves into these evolutionary stages, examining how astronomers classify stars, discussing their underlying physics, while focusing particularly on Cepheid variables.

\subsection{Discovery of Variable stars}
The year 1784 is significant in astronomy, as it was when John Goodricke observed the luminosity variation of a star in Cephus consellation - Delta Cephei - challenging the centuries-old belief that the sky is static i.e. all stars being fixed and unchanging \cite{1786john}. This finding initiated systematic searches for similar objects, leading to the identification of Cepheid variables as a distinct class.

Prior to the twentieth century, the physical origin of Cepheid variability remained uncertain. Proposed explanations included eclipsing binary systems \citep{1899pickering,1903myers,1914shapley}, magnetic activity cycles \citep{1908hale,1914russellvariables}, and tidal distortions of stellar envelopes \citep{jeans1917tidal}. However, spectroscopic observations and phase mismatches between light curves and rotation periods ruled out these hypotheses.

A decisive breakthrough was achieved by Arthur Eddington, who formulated the theory of radial stellar pulsations. Drawing an analogy with a heat engine, he introduced the $\kappa$-mechanism, in which periodic modulation of radiative opacity drives self-sustained oscillations \citep{1917eddington}. The physical basis of this mechanism was later identified by Zhevakin, who demonstrated that partial ionization of helium in the stellar envelope produces opacity variations that regulate energy flow and drive pulsation \citep{1963zhevakin}.

This section summarizes the key physical processes during different stellar evolutionary phases. It sets the foundation for understanding the physics of Cepheid pulsations and the resulting variations in their fundamental parameters.

\section{Genesis of Stars: Stellar Evolution}

\subsection{Big Bang nucleosynthesis}

\input{chapters/content/0_3_plot_quark2blackhole.tex} 

According to the Big Bang model, during the first microseconds after the origin of the Universe, matter existed as a hot, dense quark--gluon plasma within a region of only a few kilometers in horizon scale. As the Universe expanded and cooled, quarks, carrying electric charge, became confined by the strong interaction, forming hadrons. Of the six quark flavors, the lightest—up and down quarks—dominated particle formation, yielding baryons in the form of protons ($uud$, spin $1/2$) and neutrons ($udd$, spin $1/2$). 

Down quarks are slightly heavier than up quarks and can undergo beta decay, producing an up quark, an electron, and an electron antineutrino. Consequently, free neutrons ($udd$) are slightly more massive than protons (uud) and unstable, with a mean lifetime of $\sim 880$~s, decaying via 
\[
n \rightarrow p + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e,
\] 
which rapidly reduces the neutron-to-proton ratio. 

Within the first few minutes, at temperatures $T \sim 10^9$~K, Big Bang nucleosynthesis produced light nuclei like hygroden and deuterium. Once the temperature dropped below $\sim 0.1$~MeV, deuterium became stable, allowing rapid fusion into heavier nuclei. This process yielded a primordial composition of roughly $75\%$ hydrogen and $25\%$ helium by mass, with trace amounts of deuterium, ${}^3\mathrm{He}$, and ${}^7\mathrm{Li}$.

\subsection{Formation of Star}
Massive baryonic gas in dark matter potentials formed the first primordial structures. In molecular clouds, dense regions form globules composed primarily of \ce{H2} with trace heavy elements. Gravitational collapse raises core temperatures from 10–30~K, initially balanced by thermal motion and radiative cooling. As density increases, opacity traps radiation, gradually heating the core. When temperatures reach 60–100~K, thermal pressure temporarily halts the collapse, forming the first hydrostatic core. Continued mass accumulation raises the core temperature further.  

At $\sim 2000$~K, molecular hydrogen dissociates, absorbing energy and accelerating collapse until the temperature reaches $\sim 10^4$–$2\times10^4$~K, fully ionizing hydrogen and establishing the second hydrostatic core. Rotational effects produce an accretion disk, and magnetic pressure along the poles drives supersonic bipolar jets, observed as Herbig–Haro objects.

As the protostar continues to accrete mass, it contracts over the Kelvin--Helmholtz timescale, converting gravitational energy into heat. As the core temperature gradually rises to $\sim 10$~MK, two protons collide at velocities sufficient to overcome Coulomb repulsion and approach within the range of the strong nuclear force ($\sim 1$~fm). To stabilize the nucleus, one proton undergoes $\beta^+$ decay:
\[
p \rightarrow n + e^+ + \bar{\nu}_e ,
\]
corresponding at the quark level to an up quark converting into a down quark, resulting the formation of deuterium ($^2$H). This reaction initiates the proton--proton chain, releasing energy in the form of photons and neutrinos. 


Radiation pressure counter balances the continuous gravitation collapse of the matter, sustaining the spherical structure of the protostar for thousand to million years. The energy released from fusion reactions in stars calculated by Bethe and published under title - 'Energy production in stars'\citep{1939bethe}. At the heart of these calculation, Einstein's famous equality $E = \Delta m c^2$ underlies which transforms mass into energy. Here, c is the speed of light and $\Delta m$ is the difference between the mass of reactants and products of the fusion reactions. 


\subsection{Main sequence star and Turn-off point}

Stellar evolution is governed primarily by the initial stellar mass. Massive stars attain higher core temperatures and fusion rates, leading to rapid fuel consumption and short lifetimes, whereas low-mass stars burn hydrogen slowly and evolve over much longer timescales. A star’s color provides a direct proxy for its surface temperature and evolutionary state.
\input{chapters/content/0_3_note_fusion.tex}

\input{chapters/content/0_3_plot_1939_bethe.tex} 

Newly formed main-sequence stars are hydrogen-rich and sustain stable core hydrogen fusion, resulting in high surface temperatures and blue colors. As hydrogen is progressively converted into helium, an inert helium core builds up and the central fusion rate gradually declines. To maintain hydrostatic equilibrium, the core contracts while the outer envelope expands, causing the stellar radius to increase and the surface temperature to decrease. The star therefore evolves toward cooler, redder colors. This structural reconfiguration drives the star off the main sequence, defining the turn-off point in the Hertzsprung--Russell diagram. During this expansion into the red giant phase, deepening convection mixes nuclear-processed material from the interior to the surface, marking the onset of the first dredge-up. High-mass stars ($\sim 8$--$10\,M_\odot$) exhaust their nuclear fuel within $\sim 10$--$15$~Myr, whereas low-mass stars can remain on the main sequence for $\sim 15$--$20$~Gyr. Regardless of mass, stars spend the majority of their lifetimes in the main-sequence phase.


Stars with masses below $\sim 0.5\,M_\odot$ never develop sufficiently hot cores to leave the main sequence. Instead, they gradually cool and fade, evolving toward redder spectral types over timescales exceeding the age of the Universe.

\subsection{Horizontal Branch and Variable Stars}

Stars on the horizontal branch burn helium in the core and hydrogen in a surrounding shell. As they evolve toward the blue edge of the instability strip in the Hertzsprung--Russell diagram, a dynamic equilibrium develops between gravitational contraction of the envelope and thermal expansion driven by internal energy generation. This equilibrium manifests as radial oscillations of the stellar surface, producing variable stars.

The radial pulsations are primarily governed by the $\kappa$-mechanism, in which opacity variations in partially ionized layers (e.g., He\,{\sc ii}) trap and release energy, driving periodic expansion and contraction. Stars with masses roughly $0.8$--$8\,M_\odot$ can exhibit such pulsations, with periods ranging from hours to hundreds of days.

Depending on mass, temperature, and evolutionary state, different classes of pulsating stars arise:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Delta Scuti:} $1.5$--$2.5\,M_\odot$, short periods (0.02--0.3~days), radial and non-radial modes.
    \item \textbf{RR Lyrae:} $0.6$--$0.8\,M_\odot$, periods 0.2--1~day, classic radial pulsations.
    \item \textbf{Classical Cepheids:} $3$--$12\,M_\odot$, periods 1--100~days, driven by the helium $\kappa$-mechanism.
    \item \textbf{W Virginis:} $0.5$--$0.6\,M_\odot$, periods 10--30~days, low-mass Population II pulsators.
    \item \textbf{Mira Variables:} $0.8$--$8\,M_\odot$, long periods 100--1000~days, radial pulsations in the AGB phase.
\end{itemize}

These stars are particularly important in astronomy because their pulsation periods correlate tightly with intrinsic luminosity, making them reliable standard candles for distance measurements. Classical Cepheids are among the brightest, reaching absolute magnitudes of $M_V \sim -6$. The physical mechanism driving these pulsations will be discussed in the following section.

\subsection{AGB Stars and Planetary Nebulae}

Over timescales of $\sim 100$~Myr, stars evolve through the instability strip and move toward the red edge in the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram. At this stage, helium fusion continues in a shell around the carbon–oxygen core, increasing the star's luminosity and expanding it to sizes comparable to the Earth’s orbit. In HR diagram, it moves alongside the Red Giant branch which gives it name Asymptotic Giant Branch. After Helium shell runs out of fuel, outer layer of Hydrogen burning becomes the dominant source of energy and produces Helium. After 100 thousand years, Helium accumulates and ignites again spontaneously which expands the outer surface of the star even more. Expansion cools down the temperature which halts the Hydrogen shell burning. In the next hundreds of years, Helium burning stops and Hydrogen burning take place to produce more Helium. This cycle of energy production in shells along with Helium shell flash processes expel 50\% to 70\% of star's mass in the interstellar space, bringing AGB stars to its end as a planetary nebula. With Carbon and Oxygen rich core, radius of planetary nebula can reach upto 30 light years (nearest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri is about 4.2 lightyears away). 

In massive stars ($>8~M_\odot$), core temperatures exceed $3\times 10^9$~K, enabling successive burning stages of carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon, ultimately producing elements up to $^{56}$Fe, , the most tightly bound nucleus. First-generation (Population III) stars could synthesize only elements up to the third period of the periodic table, while elements heavier than iron form later through supernova nucleosynthesis or neutron-capture processes.

\subsection{Kappa Mechanism}

The kappa mechanism is a key process that drives the pulsations in Cepheid stars. 

It operates within the outer layers of the star, specifically in the partially ionized regions where the opacity, represented by the Greek letter kappa ($\kappa$), changes significantly with temperature.

The pulsations in Cepheids are characterized by radial expansion and contraction of the outer layers, causing the star to periodically brighten and dim. 

The kappa mechanism is responsible for triggering these pulsations by influencing the balance between gravity and radiation pressure in the star's interior.

    Opacity and Temperature Sensitivity: Opacity refers to the ability of a medium to absorb and scatter radiation. In Cepheid stars, the opacity is affected by the degree of ionization within the outer layers. At certain temperatures, the opacity experiences a sharp increase, leading to a localized region called the opacity bump.

Expansion and Contraction: During the pulsation cycle, the outer layers of the Cepheid star expand during maximum brightness and contract during minimum brightness. As the star expands, it cools down due to the decrease in temperature. Conversely, during contraction, the temperature increases.

Partial Ionization: As the star expands, the temperature decreases in the outer layers, causing some previously ionized atoms to recombine with electrons, resulting in partial ionization. This ionization state is crucial for the kappa mechanism to operate effectively.

Opacity Bump Effect: Within the partially ionized regions of the Cepheid star, the temperature reaches a point where the opacity is relatively high. This increased opacity affects the balance between gravity and radiation pressure. When the opacity is high, the radiation pressure becomes less effective at counteracting gravity, causing the outer layers to contract.

Energy Transfer: The contraction of the outer layers increases the temperature, reducing the degree of ionization. As a result, the opacity decreases, allowing the radiation pressure to become more efficient. This increased radiation pressure then pushes against gravity, causing the outer layers to expand.

Feedback Loop: The expansion of the outer layers leads to a decrease in temperature, triggering recombination and an increase in opacity. This increased opacity restricts radiation pressure, causing the outer layers to contract again. This cycle repeats, creating the pulsations observed in Cepheid stars.

The period-luminosity relation (also known as the Leavitt Law or the Cepheid period-luminosity relation) is an empirical relationship that exists between the pulsation period and the intrinsic luminosity of Cepheid variable stars. This relationship allows astronomers to determine the distance to Cepheids and calibrate the cosmic distance ladder.

The period-luminosity relation was first discovered by American astronomer Henrietta Leavitt in the early 20th century while studying the brightness variations of Cepheid stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud. Leavitt found that there was a consistent relationship between the periods and the average luminosities of these stars.

The period-luminosity relation states that the longer the period of a Cepheid star's pulsation, the more luminous it is. In other words, there is a direct correlation between the pulsation period and the intrinsic brightness of the star. This relationship allows astronomers to use the observed period of a Cepheid star to determine its intrinsic luminosity.

Once the intrinsic luminosity is known, the apparent brightness (or magnitude) of the star can be measured. By comparing the intrinsic luminosity with the apparent brightness, astronomers can calculate the distance to the Cepheid star using the inverse square law of light.

The period-luminosity relation is valuable because it provides a reliable and relatively straightforward method for determining distances to Cepheids and, subsequently, to other celestial objects. By measuring the period of a Cepheid star's pulsation, astronomers can estimate its intrinsic luminosity, and from that, they can determine its distance by comparing it to the observed brightness.

The period-luminosity relation has been refined over the years through extensive observations and analysis of Cepheid stars in various galaxies. It has become an essential tool for measuring cosmic distances and has played a vital role in our understanding of the scale of the universe, the expansion rate of the universe (Hubble's Law), and the calibration of other distance indicators, such as Type Ia supernovae.
