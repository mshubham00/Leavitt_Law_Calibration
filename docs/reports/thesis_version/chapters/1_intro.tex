\section{Perspective on the Subject}
\textit{Have you ever wondered why the Sun shines, why stars exhibit different colors, or what the structure of the Milky Way is? How far is the Andromeda galaxy from Earth? Do structures larger than galaxies exist? How far into the Universe can we observe? And perhaps most intriguingly, are we alone in this vast cosmos?} \footnote{Background Image: The Hubble Ultra Deep Field. Credit: Hubble Deep Field Activity (March 2009), NASA.}. 

Constrained by observational evidence yet concerned with the most fundamental questions of existence, astronomy occupies a unique position at the intersection of empirical science and philosophical inquiry, and is among the oldest intellectual pursuits of human civilization. Early reflections on the origin and nature of the Universe can be found in ancient texts, including the \emph{Nāsadīya Sūkta} (RV. X.129) of the \emph{Rigveda}, composed in Sanskrit language over 3,500 years ago. These hymns offer a speculative meditation on the primordial state of the cosmos, describing an initial condition characterized by darkness, undifferentiated existence, and a generative principle associated with heat or radiation preceding the emergence of structure and order. 

\begin{quote}
{\small 
There was neither non-existence nor existence then; \\
there was neither the realm of space nor the sky which is beyond. \\
What stirred? Where? In whose protection? \\
Was there water, bottomlessly deep? \\

There was neither death nor immortality then;\\
There was no distinguishing sign of night nor of day. \\
That One breathed, windless, by its own impulse;\\
Other than that there was nothing beyond.\\

Darkness there was at first, by darkness hidden;\\
Without distinctive marks, this all was water;\\
That which, becoming, by the void was covered;\\
That One by force of heat came into being.}
\end{quote}
\hfill -- \textit{Rigveda}~X.129.1--3, translation from \citep{2011MapsOfTime}


As an ancient conceptual precursor to modern cosmological thought, such texts reflect a profound philosophical engagement with questions concerning the origin, scale, and structure of the Universe—an inquiry that continues today through increasingly precise observations and theoretical developments. In this context, accurate measurements of cosmic distances are crucial, as they provide insights into the large-scale structure of the Universe and help address fundamental questions about its composition and evolution.  

Throughout the history of science, each new discovery has often opened the door to deeper understanding. A particularly significant milestone was the identification of pulsating stars \citep{1785pigott, 1786john} and the discovery of the strong correlation between their pulsation period and intrinsic luminosity, now known as the Leavitt Law \citep{1908leavitt}. This relationship fundamentally transformed astronomers' ability to measure cosmic distances and, consequently, to study the scale and evolution of the Universe.

\input{chapters/content/0_1_note_distance.tex}


This Master’s thesis is devoted to the subject of distance determination in astronomy, specifically focusing on the Leavitt Law — one of the primary methods used since the early twentieth century to determine extragalactic distances. This method relies on a special class of stars, Classical Cepheid variables, which pulsate radially and can be observed in distant galaxies due to their high luminosities. In this research, I utilize multiband photometric data of 121 Galactic Cepheids along with their measured distances and interstellar reddening along the line-of-sight. Using this comprehensive dataset, I demonstrate how multiband photometry can be employed to achieve high-precision estimates of distance and reddening, leading to a well-constrained Galactic Leavitt Law.

This introductory chapter provides a general background on the topic, tracing its historical development up to the present day. It also discusses how the discovery of the Leavitt Law fundamentally changed astronomers' perspectives on both the evolution and the scale of the Universe. Furthermore, this thesis is organized into three main parts, in addition to this introductory chapter. Part I comprises two chapters: the first details how key stellar parameters—such as luminosity, distance, and reddening—are derived from observational data; the second presents the physics of Cepheid pulsation and its relationship with the Leavitt Law. Part II also contains two chapters: the first explores the dataset available for calibration, and the second develops the calibration methodology to address systematic effects in distance and reddening. Part III consists of two chapters as well: the first compares the corrections suggested for two independent distance measurements (IRSB and Gaia parallax), and further verifies the calibrated distances of a sample of 17 Cepheids using an independent cluster-membership method while discussing the limitations of the current approach. The final chapter concludes with the calibrated Leavitt Law, presents its application to determine distances to the Magellanic Clouds and their three-dimensional geometries, and discusses prospects for future work, including data availability and potential extensions of the current algorithm.

\section{Spectrum of Spatial Scale}
In this section, a brief overview of the hierarchical structure of the Universe is presented, as revealed by contemporary astronomical observations. 

\input{chapters/content/0_1_tikz_spatial_scale.tex}

\subsection{Our Galaxy: Milky Way}

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_stars.tex}
To develop an intuition for cosmic scales, it is instructive to compare distances across different astronomical regimes. The nearest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri, lies at a distance of approximately 4.24 light-years \citep{Anglada2017}. By definition, one light-year is the distance traveled by light in vacuum over one year, corresponding to about 9.46 trillion kilometers. Within a radius of 10 light-years from the Sun, roughly 20 stars are known, while nearly 2,000 stars reside within 100 light-years \citep{Henry2018}. More distant stellar landmarks, such as the stars forming Orion's Belt, are located at distances ranging from approximately 800 to 1,300 light-years \citep{Bailer-Jones2018}.

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_orion.tex}

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy composed of several spiral arms. Its primary spiral arms include the Perseus Arm and the Scutum--Centaurus Arm, while secondary features include the Sagittarius Arm, the Norma Arm, and the Orion Spur \citep{Reid2014, Vallée2017}. The Solar System is located within the Orion Spur, a minor spiral feature situated between the Perseus Arm and the Scutum--Centaurus Arm, containing millions of stars \citep{Reid2019}.

Near the Galactic center, the Perseus and Scutum--Centaurus arms appear to originate close to the ends of the Milky Way’s central bar, known as the Long Bar, which extends roughly 15,000--16,000 light-years across the inner Galaxy \citep{Benjamin2005, Wegg2015}. At the very center lies Sagittarius~A*, a supermassive black hole with a mass of approximately $4 \times 10^6$~M$_\odot$, believed to mark the dynamical nucleus of the Milky Way \citep{Ghez2008, Gillessen2009}. Sagittarius~A* is located at a distance of about 27,000 light-years from Earth. Together, these components define the characteristic structure of the Milky Way: a flattened spiral disk with a central bulge and an extended stellar halo \citep{Bland-Hawthorn-2016}.

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_milkyway.tex}

\subsection{Galactic Halo and Surrounding Galaxies}

The Milky Way’s stellar disk spans approximately 100,000 light-years in diameter and is embedded within an extended, roughly spherical dark matter halo that reaches several times this scale \citep{Bland-Hawthorn2016, Sofue2013}. This halo hosts a population of satellite galaxies, stellar streams, and globular clusters \citep{McConnachie2012, Helmi2020}. Among the most prominent satellites is the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC), the most massive companion of the Milky Way, located at a distance of approximately 160,000 light-years ($\sim 50$ kpc) \citep{Pietrzynski2013, Kallivayalil2013}. The LMC itself possesses a companion, the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC), situated roughly 200,000 light-years ($\sim 60$ kpc) from Earth \citep{Harris2006}.

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_group.tex}

To date, more than 50 satellite galaxies of the Milky Way have been identified, ranging from relatively massive systems such as the Magellanic Clouds to ultra-faint dwarf galaxies \citep{2019simon}. The nearest large external galaxy is the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), located at a distance of about 2.45 million light-years ($\sim 752$ kpc) \citep{2012riess}. Andromeda is estimated to be two to three times more massive than the Milky Way \citep{2012tamm} and is expected to merge with it in 4-5 billion years \citep{2012vanDerMarel}.

Extending the observational horizon to a radius of roughly 10 million light-years reveals a population of several dozen galaxies, dominated by the Milky Way and Andromeda as the most massive members. This ensemble, known as the Local Group, constitutes a gravitationally bound system and occupies a peripheral region of the Virgo Supercluster.

\subsection{Galaxy Clusters and Superclusters}

At increasingly larger distances, the megaparsec (Mpc) becomes the standard unit for cosmological measurements, with $1 \mathrm{Mpc} \approx 3.26$ million light-years \citep{Hogg1999}. The Virgo Supercluster, which includes the Local Group, the Virgo Cluster, and numerous smaller galaxy groups, extends over a scale of approximately 30--35~Mpc (corresponding to $\sim 100$ million light-years) \citep{Tully1982, Courtois2013}.

The Virgo Supercluster itself is a constituent of the much larger Laniakea Supercluster, a vast gravitational basin encompassing a region several hundred megaparsecs across and containing on the order of $10^{5}$ galaxies \citep{Tully2014}. Prominent structures within Laniakea include the Hydra--Centaurus Supercluster, the Pavo--Indus Supercluster, and the Southern Supercluster \citep{Tully2014}.

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_voids.tex}


Near the dynamical center of Laniakea, within the Hydra--Centaurus region, lies the so-called Great Attractor—a massive concentration inferred from the peculiar velocities of galaxies \citep{Dressler1987, LyndenBell1988}. Many galaxies within Laniakea, including the Milky Way, exhibit coherent bulk flows toward this region under its gravitational influence \citep{Tully2014, Courtois2013}.

\subsection{Cosmic Web: Filaments, Walls, and Voids}


On the largest observable scales, matter in the Universe is distributed in a complex network known as the cosmic web, characterized by interconnected filaments, sheets, and voids \citep{Bond1996}. Superclusters such as Laniakea, Shapley, Hercules, Coma, and Perseus--Pisces are linked through extended filamentary structures composed of galaxies, gas, and dark matter \citep{Springel2006, Tempel2014}. One prominent example is the Pisces--Cetus Supercluster Complex, a filamentary arrangement estimated to span nearly one billion light-years in length \citep{Tully1987}.

Adjacent large-scale features include the Perseus--Pegasus Filament and other interconnected structures of comparable scale \citep{Haynes1988}. Among the most extensive reported structures is the Hercules--Corona Borealis Great Wall, which may extend over several billion light-years, although its precise size and physical coherence remain subjects of ongoing investigation \citep{Horvath2014}.

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_large_scale.tex}


Together, these filaments, sheets, and vast under-dense regions known as cosmic voids form the cosmic web, which defines the large-scale architecture of the observable Universe. This hierarchical distribution of matter is illustrated schematically in Figure~\ref{large_scale}.


\section{Historical Background and Development}

This section presents a concise overview of the historical progression of astronomical research, with particular emphasis on key discoveries and theoretical advances over the past century that have shaped contemporary cosmological thought.

\subsection{Distance Determination beyond the Galaxy: Early Twentieth Century}

Prior to the twentieth century, the Milky Way was widely believed to constitute the entirety of the Universe. The Sun was assumed to occupy a central position within this system, and the age of the Universe was thought to be far younger than current estimates \citep{Kragh1996}. Objects now recognized as external galaxies were instead interpreted as nearby gaseous clouds within the Milky Way and were collectively referred to as \textit{spiral nebulae} \citep{Sandage1981}. This limited understanding of cosmic scale culminated in a prolonged scientific controversy, most notably the Great Debate of 1920 between Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis \citep{Shapley1921, Curtis1921}.

At the heart of this debate lay the inability to measure reliable distances to spiral nebulae. A decisive breakthrough emerged with the discovery of the period--luminosity relation for pulsating variable stars, specifically Cepheid variables, by Henrietta Swan Leavitt in 1908 and its subsequent refinement in 1912 \citep{1908leavitt,1912leavitt}. She wrote:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``It is worthy of notice that in Table VI the brighter variables have the longer periods. It is also noticable that those having the longest periods appear to be as regular in their variations as those which pass through their changes in a day or two. ''}
\end{quote}


Leavitt demonstrated a tight empirical correlation between the pulsation period of Cepheids residing in SMC and their intrinsic luminosity, establishing that brighter Cepheids exhibit longer periods, see Figure~\ref{Leavitt_Hubble}. Mathematically, this relation may be expressed as
\[
M \propto \log P,
\]
where $M$ denotes the absolute magnitude and $P$ the pulsation period.

This relation provided, for the first time, a reliable standard candle for estimating distances well beyond the confines of the Milky Way. In this direction, E. Hertzsprung \citep{hertzsprung1913} and H. N. Russell \citep{russell1913} independently made the earliest attempts to calibrate the period-luminosity relation using Galactic Cepheids. Harlow Shapley \citep{shapley1918} applied the relation to determine distances to globular clusters, thereby demonstrating a larger size for the Milky Way and that the Sun does not occupy its center. Its application by Edwin Hubble \citep{hubble1925} to Cepheid variables in nearby spiral nebulae led to the unambiguous demonstration that these objects were external galaxies, vastly expanding the known scale of the Universe. By enabling the measurement of extragalactic distances, the Leavitt Law marked a turning point in astronomy, ushering in a modern understanding of the scale of the Universe.

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_Leavitt_Hubble.tex}


\subsection{Expansion of the Universe}


In 1923, using the then state-of-the-art 100-inch (2.5~m) Hooker Telescope at Mount Wilson Observatory, Edwin Hubble observed the Andromeda \textit{spiral nebula} (M31) and resolved individual Cepheid variable stars within it. By applying the period--luminosity relation for Cepheids \citep{1925hubble}, Hubble estimated the distance to M31 to be approximately 275~kpc, placing it well beyond the confines of the Milky Way. This result effectively resolved the Shapley--Curtis Great Debate in favor of Heber Curtis, demonstrating that Andromeda is an external galaxy and establishing that the Milky Way is only one among countless galaxies in the Universe.

By extending his observations to additional galaxies, Hubble subsequently uncovered a systematic relationship between distance and radial velocity: galaxies recede from the observer with velocities proportional to their distances, expressed as $v = H_0 D$, where $H_0$ is the Hubble constant \citep{1929hubble}; see Figure~\ref{Leavitt_Hubble}. This empirical law provided the first direct evidence that the Universe is undergoing a global expansion, fundamentally contradicting Einstein’s earlier static model of the Universe \citep{1917einstein}.

The Hubble constant is a cornerstone of modern cosmology, as it sets the present-day expansion rate of the Universe and provides a means to estimate its age and size. Achieving an accurate and precise determination of $H_0$ remains one of the central objectives of contemporary observational cosmology.


\subsection{Age of the Universe: 1950s}

Through detailed studies of stellar populations, Walter Baade discovered that Cepheid variables comprise two distinct classes: Type~I (classical Cepheids), which are young, metal-rich, and intrinsically brighter, and Type~II (W~Virginis stars), which are older, metal-poor, and less luminous. Baade’s recalibration of the Cepheid distance scale led to a doubling of the estimated distance to the Andromeda Galaxy and a corresponding upward revision of the inferred age of the Universe from approximately 1.8 to 3.6~billion years \citep{1958baade}.

This revision was further refined by Allan Sandage, who extended the Leavitt relation to incorporate color dependence, yielding a period--luminosity--color (PLC) relation. Sandage also revised the value of the Hubble constant to approximately $75~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ \citep{1958sandage}, resulting in an estimated cosmic age of order 14~billion years. This value is notably close to modern determinations of the Hubble constant and the age of the Universe.


\subsection{Composition the Universe: 1990s}


Adam Riess and collaborators \citep{Riess1996} standardized Type~Ia supernovae using the Phillips relation, which correlates the peak luminosity of the light curve with the rate of declining brightness \citep{1993phillips}. By calibrating extragalactic supernovae with distances derived from Cepheid variables — serving as the lower rungs of the cosmic distance ladder — this approach enabled precise distance measurements to galaxies far beyond the local Universe \citep{Riess1998, Schmidt1998}. The resulting extension of reliable distance measurements to gigaparsec scales led to one of the most surprising discoveries in modern cosmology: the expansion of the Universe is accelerating \citep{Riess1998, Perlmutter1999}. 

This acceleration implies the presence of a component with negative effective pressure that drives the expansion of spacetime. This unknown component was termed \emph{dark energy}, and despite extensive observational and theoretical efforts, its physical nature remains unknown \citep{Riess1998, Perlmutter1999}. The discovery of accelerated expansion has ushered in the era of precision cosmology and underscores the importance of understanding not only the geometry of spacetime but also the detailed composition of the Universe. A simplified description of the Universe can be framed in terms of its total mass–energy content and the geometry it induces, where energy appears in various forms such as baryonic matter, dark matter, radiation, and thermal energy \citep{dodelson2003modern}.

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_uni_composition.tex}

In the standard cosmological framework, the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model, the Universe is composed primarily of three components: baryonic matter, dark matter, and dark energy. Ordinary, or \emph{baryonic}, matter—consisting of protons, neutrons, and electrons—makes up approximately $4.9\%$ of the total energy density and forms the stars, galaxies, and large-scale structures directly observable through electromagnetic radiation \citep{2018planckVI}. Dark matter contributes about $26.8\%$ of the total and, by definition, neither emits nor absorbs electromagnetic radiation, rendering it invisible to direct observation; its existence is inferred through its gravitational influence on visible matter and radiation \citep{2018planckVI, Rubin1980}. Despite its well-established gravitational effects, the physical nature of dark matter remains one of the most significant open questions in contemporary astrophysics. Dark energy, responsible for accelerated expansion of the Universe, dominates the cosmic energy budget, contributing approximately $68.3\%$ of the total energy density of the Universe \citep{2018planckVI, Riess2018}. In contrast, all other components—including radiation, neutrinos, and possible exotic particles—together contribute by a small fraction \citep{2018planckVI}. Determining the relative proportions of these components relies heavily on precise distance measurements across cosmic scales, as such measurements underpin estimates of luminosity, redshift, and volume, all of which are essential for reconstructing the Universe’s composition and its evolutionary history.

\subsection{Cosmic Distance Ladder: The Twenty-First Century}

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_ladder.tex}

Distance measurements across cosmic scales rely on a hierarchical set of interconnected methods collectively known as the \emph{cosmic distance ladder}, in which each rung is calibrated using more local and direct techniques \citep{Hubble1929, Freedman2010}. This framework allows distances to be extended progressively to cosmological scales, where they can be compared with early–Universe inferences from the cosmic microwave background (CMB) \citep{2018planckVI}.


By calibrating SNIa luminosities with Cepheid variable stars via the Leavitt Law, distances were extended from the Cepheid-accessible local volume of $\sim 30$~Mpc ($\sim 10^8$~ly) to beyond $1000$~Mpc ($\sim 10^{10}$~ly) \citep{1998riess}. Figure~\ref{ladder} illustrates the three steps of the cosmic distance ladder. It begins with geometric parallax measurements of local Cepheid variables, which are used to calibrate the zero point of Leavitt Law. It then progresses to extragalactic Cepheids for calibrating Type~Ia supernova distances, which, when correlated with high-redshift observations, enable the determination of the Hubble constant. 

\subsection{The Space Age: Gamma, X-Ray, UV, Infrared, Microwave and Precision Astrometry}

The advent of space-based observatories enabled observations across the full electromagnetic spectrum, free from atmospheric absorption, scattering, and turbulence that severely limit ground-based measurements. Early space missions such as the \emph{Orbiting Astronomical Observatory-2} (OAO-2, 1968) and the \emph{International Ultraviolet Explorer} (IUE, 1978) opened access to the ultraviolet (UV) regime, allowing astronomers to probe high-energy stellar and interstellar processes. Observations at even shorter wavelengths became possible with X-ray missions including \emph{Uhuru} (SAS-1, 1970), the \emph{Einstein Observatory} (HEAO-2, 1978), and later the \emph{Chandra X-ray Observatory} (1999). While the first systematic study of high-energy gamma-ray emission began with the \emph{COS-B} mission in 1975.

Among these pioneering space missions, a particularly significant milestone was achieved in astrometry with the launch of the \emph{Hipparcos} (\emph{High Precision Parallax Collecting Satellite}, 1989). Hipparcos measured precise trigonometric parallaxes for approximately 120,000 stars within a distance of about 1,000 light-years ($\sim 300$ pc) \citep{1997esa}. As the first mission dedicated exclusively to high-precision astrometry, Hipparcos enabled the direct calibration of the Leavitt Law using Galactic Cepheid variables, thereby providing a fundamental rung in the cosmic distance ladder \citep{1997feats}.

\subsubsection{The Golden Era of Astronomy: Hubble, Planck, Gaia, and JWST}


The launch of the \emph{Hubble Space Telescope} (HST) in 1990 marked a pivotal advance in observational astronomy. One of its flagship programs, the \emph{Key Project on the Extragalactic Distance Scale} \citep{2001freedman}, aimed to determine the Hubble constant ($H_0$) by observing Cepheid variables in galaxies out to $\sim 25$~Mpc. The decade-long HST Key Project reported
\[
H_0 = 72 \pm 8\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
\]


Leveraging the unprecedented photometric capabilities of the \emph{Hubble Space Telescope}, the \emph{SHOES} (Supernovae, $H_0$, for the Equation of State of Dark Energy) project calibrated Type~Ia supernovae as standard candles \citep{1998riess}.  

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_cmb.tex}

Complementing local measurements, the \emph{Planck} satellite (2009) conducted an all-sky photometric and polarimetric survey of the cosmic microwave background (CMB), mapping temperature fluctuations and polarization anisotropies that encode information about early-Universe density and velocity fields \citep{2018planck,2018planckVI}. By fitting these observations to the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model, Planck team inferred
\[
H_0 = 67.4 \pm 0.5\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}},
\]



The resulting discrepancy between local and early-Universe measurements—termed the \emph{Hubble tension}—currently exceeds $5\sigma$, underscoring the need for improved calibration of the cosmic distance ladder and a deeper understanding of cosmology \citep{2022riess_tension}.

The \emph{Gaia} satellite, launched by ESA in 2013, represents a transformative advance in addressing this tension. Gaia provides microarcsecond-precision astrometry for over 1.8 billion stars upto 10 kiloparsec, yielding accurate parallaxes, proper motions, and a detailed three-dimensional map of the Milky Way \citep{2021bailer}. These measurements refine the zero-point of the Leavitt Law — a key objective aligned with the focus of this thesis. This ultimately reduces the systematic uncertainties in local distance determinations, directly enabling more precise estimates of $H_0$ through Cepheid-based calibration.

Combined with ongoing HST observations and the deployment of the \emph{James Webb Space Telescope} (JWST, 2021), Gaia firmly establishes the present era as a golden age of astronomy, characterized by multiwavelength coverage, high-precision astrometry, and transformative insights into the structure, dynamics, and evolution of the Milky Way and its surrounding.

\section{Scope of this Thesis}

It is evident from the previous sections that accurate distance measurements are essential in astronomy, as they underpin the determination of fundamental physical properties—such as size, mass, and age—across diverse astrophysical and cosmological contexts. Recognizing the importance of distance measurements in astronomy, the primary focus of my master’s thesis is the calibration of the primary distance indicator, the Leavitt Law, through the minimization of systematic errors that contribute to scatter in the period–luminosity (PL) relation.

Systematic uncertainties in reddening and distances of Galactic Cepheids directly affect the slope and zero-point of the PL relation. The effect of metallicity is not considered in this thesis. Since the PL relation forms the foundation for the next rung of the cosmic distance ladder, even small errors can propagate and amplify at larger scales. Achieving a precise and well-constrained PL relation is therefore crucial for reliable cosmological measurements. 

\begin{notebox}[sharp corners, width=\textwidth]{Aim of the Thesis.}
The core focus of this research is to understand the systematics inherent in the distance-reddening calibration method \citep{2017madore} itself, which might lead to biased in the estimated corrections. 
\end{notebox}

The refinement of the Leavitt Law is implemented through a structured methodology, summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Identify and remove outliers from the BVIJHK photometric dataset of 150 Galactic Cepheids.
    \item Refine the Galactic reddening law \citep{2007fouque} to minimize deviations between apparent and absolute Wesenheit magnitudes.
    \item Estimate true absolute luminosities of Cepheids using band-wise extinction corrections and distances derived from the Infrared Surface Brightness (IRSB) method \citep{2011storm} and Gaia parallaxes \citep{2023gaia}.
    \item Develop and adapt a mathematical framework for distance-reddening calibration based on \citep{2017madore} 
    \item Understand the systematics in Madore's approach and improve the method.
    \item Quantify uncertainties in distances and interstellar reddenings for individual Cepheids following the refined methodology.
    \item Derive the calibrated Leavitt Law using distance- and reddening-corrected true absolute magnitudes.
    \item Cross-validate the calibrated distances with Cluster Cepheids measures \citep{2023cruz}.
    \item Apply the refined Leavitt Law to Cepheids in the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds to determine their distances.
\end{itemize}

These steps are briefly outlined in the following chapters, organized into three parts of this thesis work. 

\subsection{Part I: Parameters of the Leavitt Law}
The period--luminosity (PL) relation depends on two key parameters of Cepheid variables: (a) the pulsation period and (b) the intrinsic (absolute) luminosity. Figure~\ref{PLjesper} illustrates the Leavitt Law in the $K$ band, with distances measured via the Infrared Surface Brightness (IRSB) method \citep{2011storm} and interstellar extinction estimated from Fernie's reddening measurements \citep{1994fernie} using Fouqué's Galactic extinction law \citep{2007fouque} scaled with Sandage estimation of Galactic reddening ratio in visual band \citep{2004sandage}.

\input{chapters/content/0_1_plot_PLjesper.tex}

Pulsation periods, derived from light curves spanning multiple cycles, are well-determined for all Galactic Cepheids in this study. Stellar light emitted by these stars attenuated by scattering and absorption primarily due to dust and gas along the line-of-sight. Inaccurate estimation for extinction and distance can introduce significant deviations from the expected linear period–luminosity (PL) relation. Therefore, a central objective of this work is to quantify uncertainties in both distance and reddening for each Cepheid and evaluate their impact on the PL relation. 

The first part of the thesis focuses on these parameters—distance and reddening—their interdependencies, and the physics underlying Cepheid's pulsation.

\subsection{Part II: Calibration Method and Dataset}

This part of the thesis describes the Galactic Cepheid dataset, the data cleaning process, the calibration methodology, discusses the underlying relationship between distance and reddening systematics, and performs band-wise analyses of calibrated luminosities. The dataset includes multi-band photometry in the $BVIJHK$ bands, as well as the pulsation period, distance, and reddening of individual Cepheids. The Leavitt Laws are then derived using both \textit{Gaia} parallaxes and IRSB distances. Prior to calibration, deviations in the slope and intercept of the Leavitt Law for both distance estimates are examined.

The calibration follows Madore's approach \citep{2017madore}, which employs the reddening-free Wesenheit function \citep{1982madore} to decouple reddening and distance errors. Unlike Madore, who applied a single Wesenheit relation $W_V^{VI}$ for all residual correlations, this thesis compares residuals from the $BVIJHK$ period--luminosity relations with the corresponding composite period--Wesenheit relations $W_\lambda^{VK}$. While Madore used only the $V-I$ Wesenheit function, this study extends the method to all fifteen color indices derived from $BVIJHK$ photometry, though only the $V-K$ index is used to compute the final distance--reddening error pairs for each star. Extension of Madore's method to composite Wesenheit functions demonstrates how period--Wesenheit residuals depend on reddening ratios and color indices.

\subsection{Part III: Results and Prospects for Improvement}
This part summarizes the results of the thesis using the refined Leavitt Law. To validate these results, the calibrated distances are cross-checked for 17 Galactic cluster Cepheids \citep{2023cruz}, whose distances are more reliably determined from the averaged parallaxes of their host clusters. Furthermore, the limitations of the current method are discussed, including its insensitivity to metallicity, dependence on the reddening law, and potential biases in the calibrated results due to the choice of color index.

The final chapter of the thesis presents two applications of the derived results: (i) estimating distances to the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds (LMC and SMC) using four photometric bands ($VIJK$), and (ii) enabling the determination of the 3D geometry of the Magellanic Clouds. Prospects for further improvement are also discussed.

This research utilizes Virtual Observatory (VO) services for data access, TOPCAT for catalogue analysis, and custom data-processing libraries developed in the Python programming language. The software developed as part of this work is publicly available through a GitHub repository at \url{https://github.com/mshubham00/Leavitt_Law_Calibration}.
