A \footnote{Background Image: Milky Way Central Bulge, taken from Aladin (2MASS) \citep{Bonnarel2000, Skrutskie2006}} multi-band photometric dataset of 150 Galactic Classical Cepheids (GCCs) is provided by Dr.~Jesper Storm to me for the calibration of distance and reddening estimates of individual Cepheids. The dataset includes apparent magnitudes in the $B$, $V$, $I$, $J$, $H$, and $K$ bands, obtained using different instruments at multiple observing sites \citep{2011storm}. For each Cepheid, color excess $E(B-V)$ is estimated following the method of \citep{1994fernie}. Distance moduli are derived using three independent techniques: the infrared surface brightness (IRSB) method \citep{2011storm}, Gaia parallaxes for the full sample, and cluster membership distances for 17 Cepheids associated with stellar clusters \citep{2023cruz}. The pulsation period is measured in days and transformed in logarithmic scale (not shown in the plot). 


\section{Dataset Cleaning}

The prepared dataset was collected from different sources; therefore, some Cepheids may be inconsistent in certain aspects—such as pulsation mode or photometric errors—which can affect data calibration. Identifying and filtering out such outliers is the first step.

\subsection{Overtone Pulsation mode}
In the given dataset, seven Cepheids were identified as overtone pulsators and were therefore excluded from the sample. These are \textbf{SU Cas, V0465 Mon, QZ Nor, DT Cyg, EV Sct, FF Aql, and SZ Tau}. The final dataset, consisting of 143 Galactic Classical Cepheids pulsating in the fundamental mode, is presented in Appendix A of this thesis.

\subsection{Photometric error}
Cepheids with inconsistent photometric measurements must be removed from the dataset. To identify such outliers, the period–color relation is employed. This approach is effective because both the intrinsic color and the period of a Cepheid are independent of distance. Since a Cepheid’s period is strongly correlated with its luminosity, plotting period against color reproduces key features of the color–magnitude diagram, particularly the region known as the instability strip.

Within the instability strip, short-period Cepheids occupy the lower region, while long-period (and thus intrinsically brighter) Cepheids lie toward the upper region. Cepheids that significantly deviate from the linear trend defined by the instability strip are likely affected by inaccurate photometry in specific bands and are therefore considered outliers. In general, the intrinsic color index for any pair of photometric bands can be calculated as shown in Equation~\ref{color}.
\begin{align}
	(m_1-m_2)_0 = (m_1-m_2) - (R_1^{BV} - R_2^{BV}) E_{BV}
	\label{color}
\end{align}

From the BVIJHK photometry, fifteen color combinations were constructed. Tracing the anomalous behavior of individual GCCs in each color–period diagram helps identify inconsistencies in specific photometric bands. Cepheids exhibiting such anomalous behavior are listed in Table~\ref{outliers}.

\subsection{PL/PW Residuals}
An additional set of outliers was identified during the calibration process. To calibrate individual GCCs for distance and reddening errors, the residuals from the Period–Luminosity (PL) and Period–Wesenheit (PW) relations were correlated. The slope of the resulting regression line plays a central role in the calibration procedure.

In this process, nine Cepheids (\textbf{CY Aur, RX Cam, V1344 Aql, RW Cam, SV Per, MW Cyg, RZ Gem, V0495 Cyg, and VX Per}) were found to have large residuals that significantly affected the slope of the regression line compared to the rest of the sample. These objects were therefore excluded from the analysis, ultimately leaving 121 Cepheids in the cleaned dataset.

\section{Extinction Law Calibration}

To calibrate the reddening–distance error, the pulsation period of a Cepheid plays a key role, as it is a well-observed and highly precise parameter for each star. For Galactic Classical Cepheids (GCCs), the period determines the true absolute magnitude across the spectral range through the Period–Luminosity relation, thereby enabling the decoupling of distance and extinction effects from the observed apparent magnitude.

Unlike distance, extinction cannot be measured directly. However, its wavelength-dependent variation can be quantified through the color excess. For the dataset, color excess is measured between the B and V bands, defined as $E(B-V) = A_B - A_V $. By scaling with the total-to-selective extinction ratio, the color excess can be converted into absolute extinction, for the V band, $R_V \times E(B-V) = A_V$. The reddening ratio is related to the properties of interstellar dust grains and increases with particle size. For the Milky Way, a typical value is $R_V = 3.23$  \cite{2004sandage}. For other photometric bands, the corresponding total-to-selective extinction ratios can be derived using an extinction law, $R_V \times \frac{A_\lambda}{A_V} = R_\lambda$ \cite{1989cardelli}. 

In his calibration method, Madore \cite{2017madore} adopted the extinction law presented in Table \ref{fouque} \citep{2007fouque} to derive the $(V - I)$-based Wesenheit magnitudes for 59 GCCs. The Wesenheit function, $W= m - R(m_1 - m_2)$, is sensitive to the adopted composite reddening ratio, which implies that errors in the extinction law or in the visual reddening ratio contribute directly to the residuals of the Period–Wesenheit relation. Therefore, it is crucial to minimize such systematic uncertainties prior to performing the distance–reddening calibration for the GCC dataset.

Since the Wesenheit magnitude does not require an independent measurement of color excess and is equivalent to the unreddened magnitude, $W = W_0$, the available color excess measurements can be leveraged to compute deviations between \(W\) and \(W_0\). These deviations can then be plotted for the dataset to assess residual reddening effects. Noticing the non-zero deviation, it can be said that the deviation, $\delta W$, can be due to following equation:

$$\delta W = \delta A_\lambda - \delta R \times E(m_1-m_2) $$

For the given dataset, I adjusted the reddening and extinction ratios to different values (see Table \ref{exlaw}), for which the deviation between $W$ and $W_0$ is minimized.

Such fine adjustments to the reddening ratio and extinction law for the considered dataset yield a smaller deviation between $W$ and $W_0$, thereby minimizing the systematic error in the residuals of the period–Wesenheit magnitude.

\section{Data Transformation}

After cleaning the outliers, the dataset can be used to calibrate the Leavitt Law. The next step is to transform the observed apparent magnitudes into true apparent magnitudes using the distance modulus and color-excess measurements.

This transformation is illustrated in the adjacent plot, Figure \ref{magcom}. In the plot, the period (x-axis) is plotted against luminosity (y-axis). The apparent magnitudes in the B and K bands are shown with red dashed lines, while the remaining bands—V, I, J, and H—lie between the two edges of the colored region. After band-wise extinction correction, the resulting true apparent magnitudes are represented by the magenta region.
\begin{align}
	m_\lambda^0 = m_\lambda - R_\lambda \times E_{BV}
	\label{true_apparent}
\end{align}

\input{chapters/content/0_4_plot_magcom.tex} 

Between red and magenta region, the shift in the B band is much larger than in the K band, as light at shorter wavelengths is much more sensitive to extinction compared to longer wavelengths. After extinction correction, the resulting magnitudes become brighter, and the red region is compressed into the brighter and narrower magenta region. At this stage, no clear correlation between period and luminosity is evident.

The next step adjusts the distance for each Cepheid, transforming the true apparent magnitudes into true absolute magnitudes, as represented by the yellow region (Gaia based distance used in the Figure \ref{magcom}).
\begin{align}
	M_\lambda = m_\lambda^0 - \mu
	\label{true_absolute}
\end{align}

At this stage, a linear trend emerges between the period and intrinsic luminosity of the Cepheids, known as the Leavitt Law.

Another version of the extinction-corrected luminosity (true absolute magnitude) is the reddening-free magnitude, derived through the Wesenheit function \citep{1982madore}. An important feature of the Wesenheit magnitude is that it does not require a direct measurement of the color excess; instead, it depends on the color index and reddening ratio. For multiband photometry (BVIJHK), I considered all fifteen combination of color indices to investigate the key differences among them. In Figure \ref{magcom}, the cyan and green regions represent two families of Wesenheit magnitudes—HK and JH, respectively. The lower and upper edges of these regions correspond to the B and K bands, while the shaded region encompasses the remaining four bands used in the Wesenheit magnitude. The other thirteen families of Wesenheit magnitudes lie between the HK and JH regions and are explicitly shown in Figure \ref{magwes}.

It is evident that not all color bases for the Wesenheit magnitude yield the same result, indicating that Wesenheit magnitudes are highly sensitive to the choice of color index. A smaller difference between the two edges makes it a better choice for calibration, as all Wesenheit bands converge to a reddening-free magnitude. Based on the observation, V–K-based Wesenheit magnitudes exhibit smaller variation across the bands while covering a larger observational wavelength range. Therefore, I adopted the V–K color index for distance–reddening calibration, instead of the V–I color as used by \cite{2017madore},.

\section{Galactic Leavitt Law}
The periodic range of 121 Cepheids lies between 0.43 and 1.8 on a logarithmic scale, near to their mean, 1 is chosen as the pivot point and subtracted from the period, $\log P $,  before calculating the regression coefficients, $\alpha_\lambda$ and $\beta_\lambda$. The 'true' absolute magnitude is calculated using Equation \ref{true_absolute}. In its most generic form, the linear fit between period-luminosity relation is expressed as follows:
\begin{align}\label{eq:plr}
	M_\lambda & = \alpha_\lambda (\log P -1) + \beta_\lambda 
\end{align}

The Figure \ref{pl} provides a comparative overview of the Leavitt Laws across different bands. Note the decreasing width of the period–luminosity (PL) relation from the B band to the K band. As the distance remains fixed for individual Galactic Cepheids across the bands, the variation among the bands arises primarily from extinction correction and, to a lesser extent, from the metallicity of the stars. The increasing Pearson correlation coefficient indicates a declining effect of extinction at longer wavelengths. Assuming that Cepheids strictly follow the PL relation, the residuals in each PL relation therefore contain information about the uncertainties in reddening or distance measurements.

\section{Wesenheit variants}

The period–Wesenheit (PW) relations exhibit relatively higher correlation coefficients than their corresponding period–luminosity (PL) relations, as Wesenheit magnitudes are independent of extinction-related errors. Being reddening-free, the scatter in the Wesenheit-based Leavitt Law arises primarily from uncertainties in distance and in the adopted reddening ratio, plausible effect of metallicity is ignored in this work. In his method of decoupling reddening and distance errors \citep{2017madore}, Madore utilized a V–I-based Wesenheit magnitude, adopting a reddening ratio of $R_V = 3.23$ \citep{2004sandage}, shown in the first panel of Figure \ref{irsbpwvk}. Following panels depict $V-K$ based period-wesenheit relations for Gaia and IRSB distance respectively. 
\begin{align}\label{eq:plw}
	W_\lambda^{12} &  = \alpha_\lambda^{12} (\log P -1) + \beta_\lambda^{12}
\end{align}
